\section{Tier 5 --- mixtures, multimodality, and hierarchical geometry}
\label{sec:tier5}

The hardest structured tier: targets that are mixtures or behave like mixtures (mixture
models, latent-variable models, label switching, hierarchical models, Bayesian neural
networks). Here the constants are governed not by curvature inside a mode but by the
\emph{barriers between modes}, and a finite constant can still be astronomically large.

\begin{theorem}[Separated mixtures have exponentially large constants]
\label{thm:separated-mixture-lower}
For a mixture or multi-well potential whose wells are separated by an energy barrier of height
$B$ --- the potential gap any path between the wells must climb --- the constants grow at least
exponentially in the barrier, $\CP,\CLS\ge e^{cB}$. The bottleneck is exhibited by a
mode-indicator test function (smoothly $0$ on one well, $1$ on the other): its Rayleigh
quotient is large because the gradient energy concentrates on the thin, low-density inter-mode
region. For Gaussian-type wells the barrier grows quadratically in the \emph{separation
distance} $\Delta$ between the modes, $B\asymp\Delta^2$, so the growth reads $e^{c\Delta^2}$ in
the separation; but the intrinsic dependence is linear in the barrier height, and a steeper
(e.g.\ quartic) landscape makes $B$ grow faster than $\Delta^2$.
\end{theorem}
\noindent\cite{BakryGentilLedoux2014,MenzSchlichting2014}. This kills Holley--Stroock here
(between separated wells $\osc(W)=\infty$, Theorem~\ref{thm:holley-stroock}). The benign
counterpart is the bounded-$\chi^2$ regime of Corollary~\ref{cor:ccnw-mixture-lsi}:
multimodality does not automatically kill the inequalities, but separation makes the
constants enormous.

\begin{family}[One-dimensional double well]
\label{fam:double-well-1d}
For $\Pstar\propto\exp\!\big(-(x^2-a^2)^2/4\big)$ on $\R$, the naive ``$1+a^2$'' Poincar\'e
upper bound is \emph{falsified} by mode-indicator Rayleigh quotients once the wells at $\pm a$
are well separated; the true constant grows exponentially in the barrier
(Theorem~\ref{thm:separated-mixture-lower}).
\end{family}
\noindent A concrete $1$D witness: the matching upper bound needs the energy-landscape
decomposition of \cite{MenzSchlichting2014} or the $1$D Hardy evaluator
(Theorem~\ref{thm:hardy-1d}).

\begin{remark}[Label switching is a symmetry artifact, not physical multimodality]
\label{rem:label-switching}
An exchangeable $K$-component mixture posterior is invariant under component relabeling. A free
orbit of a non-collision mode has $K!$ representatives, but symmetry alone neither guarantees
$K!$ distinct modes nor forces the raw Poincar\'e constant to be terrible. Passing to
$\Theta/S_K$ never worsens the constants (Theorem~\ref{thm:a5-monotone}); it improves $\CP$
strictly exactly when the non-invariant spectral block is slower than the invariant block
(Proposition~\ref{prop:a5-ratio}). Thus quotienting removes symmetry-induced bottlenecks but not
permutation-invariant physical multimodality.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Hierarchical funnels and reparameterization]
\label{rem:funnel}
Hierarchical models create ``funnels'': for $\tau\sim p(\tau)$, $\theta_j\mid\tau\sim N(0,\tau^2)$,
the joint density has regions where $\tau$ is small and the $\theta_j$ are extremely
concentrated, producing severely anisotropic, position-dependent curvature that no global floor
captures. A functional-inequality question with direct sampling consequences is how
$\CP,\CLS,\CTCI$ transform under centered, non-centered, and partially non-centered coordinates
after fixing a common normalization. Full non-centring removes the prior-only Neal funnel, but it
is not uniformly better: the reviewed Gaussian calibration has a data-strength crossover, and
centering wins in the strong-data regime (Section~\ref{sec:a5}).
\end{remark}

\noindent For nonconvex losses (Bayesian neural networks) the same dichotomy governs:
local curvature inside basins helps and barriers between basins hurt. A Gaussian prior controls
the tails, while finiteness and usefulness of the constants still require the appropriate
regularity and no-bottleneck hypotheses; high barriers can make finite constants exponentially
large.
